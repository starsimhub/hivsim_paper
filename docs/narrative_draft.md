# Narrative draft (Robyn, 2026-10-23)

Model comparison exercises have historically been both highly desirable in theory and very difficult in practice. The HIV modeling landscape is particularly active in this space: given the many well-validated models that exist across the globe, there is clear value in understanding what each of them has to say about various epidemiological and policy questions. Over the past two decades, model comparison papers have become an established part of the HIV modeling field, much of it coordinated through the HIV Modelling Consortium. Early exercises were driven by policy: models were used to inform voluntary medical male circumcision rollout (UNAIDS/WHO/SACEMA Expert Group, 2009), and twelve independent models simulated standardised antiretroviral therapy (ART) scale-up scenarios in South Africa (Eaton et al., 2012). Later work used the same approach to examine how model structure shapes projections (Hontelez et al., 2013), to compare costs and cost-effectiveness (Eaton et al., 2014; Keebler et al., 2014), to assess pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and drug resistance (van de Vijver et al., 2013), and to validate projections retrospectively against new national survey data (Eaton et al., 2015). More recent comparisons have covered the effects of COVID-19 service disruptions (Jewell et al., 2020), interventions to improve retention on ART (Bershteyn et al., 2022), long-acting injectable PrEP (Stansfield et al., 2023; Stansfield et al., 2025), gaps in the treatment cascade (Bansi-Matharu et al., 2025), and the identification of priority populations, the latter drawing on 15 models from ten African countries (Silhol et al., 2026). Several have quantified the consistency of projections at the country level directly (Moolla et al., 2023; Taramusi et al., 2025). Together these efforts have produced formal good-practice guidelines for multi-model comparisons (den Boon et al., 2019).

Despite these successes, model comparisons remain difficult to interpret. Independent models calibrated to the same surveillance and survey data typically reproduce the observed quantities closely: population size, HIV prevalence, and numbers diagnosed and on ART. Yet they diverge on the unobserved quantities that policy actually depends on. In a recent comparison conducted through the Modelling to Inform HIV Programmes in Sub-Saharan Africa (MIHPSA) Collaboration, six established models were well aligned on population and prevalence in Malawi, Zimbabwe and South Africa (Bansi-Matharu et al., 2025). Yet in Malawi and Zimbabwe their estimates of new infections in 2024 ranged from fewer than 10,000 to more than 30,000, and they attributed between 30% and 65% of Zimbabwe’s transmissions to undiagnosed people (Bansi-Matharu et al., 2025). A later analysis found that the share of HIV deaths among people on ART ranged from 35% to 64% across models (Bansi-Matharu et al., 2026). This pattern is long-standing. In the ART comparison conducted in Eaton et al (2012), the models broadly agreed on short-term impact but diverged on long-term outcomes such as the prospect of elimination. Those differences could not be traced to the structural or parameter choices hypothesised to drive them. Formally, this is a problem of non-identifiability, or “equifinality.” Calibration targets constrain only a few combinations of inputs, so many parameter sets, and many model structures, fit the data equally well while implying different counterfactuals and different optimal decisions (Beven, 2006; Gutenkunst et al., 2007; Alarid-Escudero et al., 2018). For example, a range of assumptions about the share of transmission during early infection are consistent with South African prevalence data, and that share does not reliably predict the long-term impact of treatment (Eaton & Hallett, 2014). Direct empirical estimates of early-infection infectiousness are themselves contested (Bellan et al., 2015).

Structural divergence among calibrated models is a source of uncertainty that requires explicit methodological treatment. Most commonly, structural disagreement is treated as a formal part of uncertainty, to be characterised and propagated rather than explained away. Tools for this include model averaging, explicit model-discrepancy terms, and structured multi-model ensembles (Kennedy & O’Hagan, 2001; Bilcke et al., 2011; Jackson et al., 2011; Brynjarsdóttir & O’Hagan, 2014; den Boon et al., 2019; Shea et al., 2020). Second, one can test whether the disagreement actually matters for the decision. In Ebola and foot-and-mouth disease, models that differed widely in projected caseloads still ranked interventions consistently (Probert et al., 2016; Li et al., 2017). Value-of-information analysis can then show which unobserved quantities, if measured, would most change the recommended policy (Strong et al., 2014; Jackson et al., 2022). Third, new data can target the disputed quantities directly, rather than adding more targets that the models already fit. Population-level deep-sequence phylogenetics, for instance, now gives empirical estimates of who transmits to whom (Ratmann et al., 2019; Monod et al., 2024). Fourth, the assumptions responsible for the divergence can be isolated by varying them one at a time in a controlled setting. Hontelez et al. (2013) did this by building nine models of increasing complexity in a stepwise approach. That approach is currently very costly, because each assumption is embedded in a separate, independently maintained codebase.

A more ambitious alternative would make model differences decomposable rather than merely describable. Decomposable in this sense implies that each established model could be re-expressed within a common framework as a set of interchangeable components: demography, sexual network and partnership formation, natural history and infectiousness, the testing and treatment cascade, interventions, and calibrated parameter values. The divergence between any two models could then be traced through a sequence of controlled substitutions. Starting from model A, one would replace its components one at a time with those of model B (for example, running PopART-IBM with EMOD’s sexual network) and record the change in each policy-relevant output at every step. Once every component has been exchanged, the hybrid is model B and should reproduce its results. That endpoint serves both as a verification test of the re-implementation and as a guarantee that the stepwise changes sum to the total difference between the two models. The idea extends earlier work. Hontelez et al. (2013) built a stepwise sequence of increasingly complex models, and Kuntz et al. (2011) and van Ballegooijen et al. (2011) designed controlled “model experiments” to expose the unobserved assumptions behind divergent colorectal cancer screening projections. More broadly, it echoes the long-standing practice of “docking” independently built simulations to establish whether, and why, they agree (Axtell et al., 1996). Because components interact, the contribution attributed to any single substitution depends on the order in which substitutions are made. Implementing this requires a framework in which disease modules, networks, demographic processes and interventions are separable, share common interfaces, and can be swapped without rewriting the surrounding model. Low-variance comparison of stochastic runs, for instance through common random numbers, also matters, so that small deltas are not swamped by simulation noise.

HIVsim, built on the modular Starsim architecture, is designed with this goal in mind. In its envisaged form, the question “why do these models disagree?” would no longer be met with a qualitative appeal to structural differences. Instead it would be answered by a quantitative account of how much each modelling choice contributes to the gap.
